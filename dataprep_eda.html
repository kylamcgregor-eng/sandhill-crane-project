DataPrep/EDA

Understanding when and where sandhill cranes appear requires observations that span many years, many places, and a range of weather conditions. This page documents where each dataset came from, how it was gathered, how it was cleaned, and what the cleaned data look like. Everything on the page can be reproduced by running the three Python scripts linked below, in order.

Data currency. The data were pulled on September 20, 2026. GBIF's copy of the eBird dataset contains no records for 2025 yet, so the historical study period is 2010 to 2024, the eBird API extends the record to the present, and land cover is available for six dates between 2008 and 2021.

1. Data sources

Six sources were combined. Five come from web services and APIs (Application Programming Interfaces) accessed with Python, and one was created by hand. Bird sightings come from two of those services, weather comes from NOAA station records and from the Open-Meteo weather-model service, and land cover comes from the U.S. Geological Survey.

Code

All scripts are written in Python 3 and use the requests, pandas, numpy, matplotlib, tifffile, and pyproj packages (see requirements.txt). They run in order and share settings through config.py.

  • 00_intro_schematic.py – draws the schematic used in the Introduction.
  • 01_gather_data.py – pulls data from the APIs and web services, chooses the NOAA weather stations, retrieves the land-cover maps, and writes the raw files.
  • 02_clean_data.py – cleans, discretizes, and normalizes; writes the clean files and a cleaning log.
  • 03_eda_figures.py – produces the figures and data previews on this page.

3. Cleaning and preparation

Cleaning and visual exploration were done together: each figure below either exposed a data problem or confirmed that one had been fixed. All decisions are implemented in 02_clean_data.py, and every filtering step is counted in cleaning_log.json.

Steps applied to the sightings

  • Duplicates. Records with a repeated GBIF identifier were removed.
  • Missing or invalid location and date. Records without a usable date or coordinates were removed, and so were coordinates outside North America (latitude 14–72, longitude −170 to −50).
  • Vague locations. Records whose reported coordinate uncertainty exceeded 10 km were removed, because they cannot be mapped or matched to weather reliably.
  • Presence-only reports. eBird allows an “X” when a species is present but not counted. These arrive with no count and were removed, since later models need a number of birds. The share removed is charted in the figure on presence-only reports.
  • Impossible counts. Counts below 1 or above 500,000 birds in a single report were removed. Large but plausible flocks were kept and flagged instead (see below).
  • Missing state or province. Filled with “Unknown” so the column stays complete.
  • Site-area records. The same steps were applied to the site-area pull. Each remaining record was then kept only if it lay within 60 km of its nearest reference site, which removes the corners of the search boxes and the copies of records that fall inside the overlapping boxes of the two Platte River sites.

Steps applied to the weather and site tables

  • Station choice. For each reference site, the nearest GHCN-Daily station within 60 km (widened to 100 km if needed) that reports maximum temperature, minimum temperature, and precipitation from 2010 through 2024 was selected. First-order weather stations, and stations that also report snow and wind, were favored. A site with no suitable station is left out of the weather table. The stations chosen are listed in ncei_stations_used.csv.
  • Required values. Days missing maximum temperature, minimum temperature, or precipitation were removed, along with physically impossible values (a maximum below the minimum, temperatures beyond ±60 °C, or negative precipitation, snow, or wind).
  • Daily mean temperature. Calculated as the average of the daily maximum and minimum, because the station average temperature field is blank at many stations.
  • Snow and wind gaps in the station data. Snow depth and snowfall blanks in June through September were set to zero because snow does not fall then, and other gaps of up to three days were filled by linear interpolation. A station variable was kept only if every site had at least 85 percent of its days filled. Snowfall (77 percent at the weakest site), snow depth (75 percent), and station wind (reported at only two of the seven stations) all fell short, so none of them was kept from NCEI.
  • Snow and wind from Open-Meteo. Because those station variables were dropped, snowfall and wind, snow depth, were taken from the Open-Meteo reanalysis instead. Duplicate days, days with a missing value, and physically impossible values (negative snow or wind, a gust below the sustained wind, or a wind direction outside 0 to 360 degrees) were removed, snowfall was converted from centimeters to millimeters, and hourly snow depth was reduced to the daily maximum. The result was joined to the station table by site and date, and only days with both sources were kept. The temperature and precipitation columns still come from the stations. As a check, the model’s daily mean temperature and the station-based value agree closely (a correlation of 0.96, with the model averaging 0.3 °C warmer).
  • Crane reports per site. The cleaned site-area records were summed by site and day into the number of reports, the total cranes counted, and the largest single count. Days with no counted report at a site were recorded as zero reports, not as missing, because an unreported day is the absence of observers as much as the absence of cranes. Site-days with reports but no usable weather were removed, and a has_records column keeps the zero-versus-reported distinction visible.

Steps applied to the land-cover and effort tables

  • Pixel counts to shares. Each 30 m pixel of the NLCD map carries one of 16 land-cover codes. Codes were combined into groups (developed, cropland, pasture and hay, grassland and shrub, forest, wetland, open water, barren), and each 5 km cell’s pixel counts were turned into percentages of that cell.
  • Complete cells only. Cells cut off at the edge of a map (fewer than 95 percent of their pixels) and cells whose center is more than 60 km from the site were removed, so every cell is a full square inside the site area.
  • Land-cover change. For each cell the 2011 shares were subtracted from the 2021 shares, giving the change in percentage points. The 2011 and 2021 maps were chosen because they fall closest to the start (2010–2014) and the end (2020–2024) of the bird record.
  • Crane reports per cell. Cleaned site-area sightings were placed in the same 5 km cells and counted for the early and late periods. Each cell’s share of its site’s reports in each period was calculated so that the overall growth in reporting does not hide where cranes were reported. Cells with no reports keep a value of zero, and cells with fewer than five crane reports in total are flagged (enough_reports) rather than deleted.
  • Observer access. The number of eBird hotspots (public birding locations) in each cell was added, because cranes cannot be reported where nobody goes.
  • Reporting effort. Crane records per year at each site were divided by all eBird records of any species in the same area and year, giving crane records per 1,000 eBird records.

Known limitations

  • Sampling. The continent-wide sample used for the maps and distributions reads at most 600 records from each country-year-month to keep the raw file a manageable size. The site-area pull is different: it contains every record GBIF holds inside each site's search box, so daily counts at a site are complete for the records GBIF has. GBIF returns records in a fixed order rather than at random, so randomly placed slices approximate, but do not perfectly equal, a random sample. The true record totals for each month are stored in the raw file (cell_total_records) and are used for the reporting-effort charts.
  • Study period. GBIF's copy of the eBird dataset had no 2025 records when the data were pulled, so the historical record ends in December 2024.
  • Overlapping reports. Several observers often report the same flock, so the total cranes counted in a day can add the same birds more than once. The largest single count that day is the safer measure of flock size, and the daily total is best treated as an index of reporting.
  • Presence-only days. A day with only presence-only reports is treated as a day with no counted report, even though cranes were seen.
  • Land-cover resolution. NLCD classifies 30 m squares into broad classes, so small restoration projects, changes inside a river channel, and shifts between crop types are mostly invisible, and only six dates between 2008 and 2021 are available.
  • Land cover is not the whole story. The tests compare cells and sites, not individual birds, and many things that matter to cranes, such as river flows, disturbance, and waste grain, are not in the land-cover maps. Any pattern found is an association and not proof of cause.
  • Observer effort. Sightings come from volunteers, so places and months with more birdwatchers produce more reports. Counts of reports are not counts of birds.
  • Site coordinates. Reference-site coordinates are approximate and the two sites added beyond those named in the Introduction should be verified before being cited.
  • Modelled snow and wind. Open-Meteo values are a weather-model reconstruction on a grid of roughly 10 km to 25 km, so they smooth over local conditions, can understate snow depth and gusts at a particular field or river bend, and are not readings from an instrument. The grid point used for each site sits a few kilometers from the coordinates requested.
  • Station distance. A weather station can be tens of kilometers from the site it represents, so its readings approximate, rather than equal, conditions at the birds' location.
  • Recent API window. The eBird API reaches back only 30 days, so its time series begins on the date of the first pull.

5. Land use and cranes: a first look

Research on the Central Platte River Valley found that cranes moved earlier in the spring and increasingly concentrated in the eastern part of the valley while river channels and land cover were changing (U.S. Geological Survey). That raised the question of whether land-use change, such as development or restored wetland, also shows up in the sightings collected here. A first check used the National Land Cover Database, which classifies every 30-meter square of the lower 48 states into land-cover classes, at six dates around each of the seven reference sites.

Three results stand out. First, land cover barely changed within 60 km of any site between 2011 and 2021 (figure “Land-cover change around each site”). Second, in the 5 km cells where land use did change, crane reports did not move in a consistent direction. The 17 analyzed cells that gained at least one percentage point of developed land held about 41 percent of the analyzed crane reports in 2010–2014 and about 43 percent in 2020–2024, and none of the 69 cells that had crane reports early but none late had gained that much developed land. The 11 cells with the largest wetland gains showed no larger rise in crane share than other cells (a median change of +0.28 versus +0.25 percentage points). Third, where cranes are reported is strongly tied to water: cranes were reported in far more cells with open water or wetland, especially in cells with an eBird hotspot.

These results do not show that development or restoration has no effect on cranes. The land-cover maps cannot see small restoration projects or changes inside a river channel, only a small number of cells changed much (17 gained at least one percentage point of developed land), and the tests are associations across cells rather than tests of cause. The share of all eBird records that were cranes also roughly doubled at the two Platte River sites between 2010 and the mid-2010s, and land cover at this scale cannot explain that; it may reflect changing observer behavior, the eastward shift described by the U.S. Geological Survey, or habitat features that NLCD does not measure. Later modules will use models that account for reporting effort and can bring in additional habitat data.
